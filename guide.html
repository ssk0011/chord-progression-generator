How to use Chord Changes

Chord Changes helps you find chord progressions, hear them, understand why they work and take them into your music app. You don’t need to know music theory to start; the theory is there when you want it.

Quick start

  1. Bring your chords, or get some. The first time you open it, the chart holds a short example to show the colours. In Create → I have chords, type your own (C Am F G, I vi IV V or 1 6m 4 5), tap them from the row of chords in your key, or play them on a MIDI keyboard. With no chords in mind, open Generate, choose a mood and genres, and press Generate.
  2. Read what you have. Under the chart, a sentence or two says what the progression does: its key, which chords come from outside it and why, and where it ends. Ideas for this progression offers three swaps from across the chart.
  3. Change anything you don’t like. Tap a chord to hear it, then try one of the swap ideas, or tap the small ▾ next to its name to pick a different chord.
  4. Take it with you. Press Export to download MIDI or notation, copy the chord chart, or print a lead sheet.

Simple view: for beginners

New to chords? Press Simple view (bottom of the left rail on a computer, top bar on a phone). It hides the songwriting tools and teaches as you go. Press it again for the full app; your chart stays as it is.

  • Four jobs instead of theory colours. Every chord is Home (green: where the song rests), Away (blue: it travels), Pull (pink: it leans back toward home) or Surprise (yellow: from outside the key). Its job is written under its name, and the Chord panel says in a sentence what it does, such as “D is Pull in G major: it leans back toward G, and your ear wants G next.”
  • What comes after? Under the chart, three choices for the next chord, one Home, one Away and one Pull, each saying what the move does (“Pull → Home: the strongest move in music”). ▶ plays the move; Add puts the chord after the selected one and moves on to it, so you can build a song one chord at a time. A chord that pulls somewhere other than home (like B7 in G, which leans to Em) offers that chord as its next step.
  • Plain chords. Generate and the chord row use major and minor triads only, with the guitar shapes and piano voicings for each. Your Chords allowed setting comes back when you leave Simple view.
  • Show me the theory (next to the colour key) adds the Roman numerals and the theory name to every chord and sentence, as a bridge to the full app.

Colours, next to the chart title (or Change the colours under the colour key), sets how the chords are coloured, in either view:

  • Colour the chords by Where it’s from (in the key, borrowed, secondary, chromatic) or Its job (Home, Away, Pull, Surprise). In the full app, numerals and every tool stay as they are, and the Chord panel adds the chord’s job in a sentence.
  • Your colours: a swatch for each colour, to choose your own. Chord names switch to white on dark colours so they stay readable. Reset colours goes back to the starting ones.
  • Home follows the key (with Its job): give each of the 12 keys a colour, and Home chords take the colour of the key they’re in, so after a key change the new key’s Home chords change colour too. The starting key colours are just an even spread round the circle of fifths, C blue; they don’t claim any key “is” a colour, so make them yours.

All of it is remembered in this browser, and Undo leaves it alone.

In theory terms the jobs are the three functions: Home is the tonic (I, iii, vi), Away the subdominant (ii, IV), and Pull the dominant (V, vii°, and secondary dominants). Undo leaves the view alone.

Easier to read

  • Text size (the Aa button beside Simple view) switches between Normal, Large and Largest. Everything grows together, text and the buttons around it, and the layout rearranges itself to fit rather than spilling off the side. The choice is remembered. Even at Normal, no text is smaller than 12px, apart from the short part names inside the arrangement lanes.
  • Focus (beside Colours above the chart, or the \ key) hides the menus and the side panel so the chart fills the window; the Play bar stays. Press Show panels or \ again to bring them back. It’s remembered, and anything you open in a side panel brings the panels back by itself. Phones show one screen at a time already, so they don’t have it.
  • Zooming with your browser (⌘/Ctrl and +) works too. When the window gets short, as it does when you zoom a laptop in, the Play bar shrinks to one row (Play, tempo, More, Export) so the chart keeps most of the screen; More shows loop, metronome, sound and style. The same one-row bar is used with Large or Largest text.
  • Buttons you press are at least 36px tall, and grow with the text size.

Finding your way around

Can’t find something? Press Search at the top of the menu (or ⌘/Ctrl + K, or /) and type what you’re after in your own words: “tempo”, “capo”, “MIDI”, “print”, “bigger text”. Pick a result and the app either does it (Play, Undo, Focus, the downloads) or takes you to the control, opening whatever menu or panel holds it and ringing it in yellow. Help topics open in the guide.

Making a progression

From your own chords

Open Create → I have chords (it is where Create opens until you pick another tab; it remembers the last one).

  • Type chords as names (Am7 D/F# Bbmaj7), Roman numerals (I vi ii7 V7, V7/V, ♭VII, viiø7) or Nashville numbers (1 6m 4 5, 2m7 5/7, b7). Upper-case numerals are major and lower-case minor; a Nashville number is major unless you add m or -. Numerals count from the major scale of the key, as the chart’s own numerals do, so in A minor C is ♭III or b3. Before you press Add, the line under the box says how it reads them (“Reads as G Em C A7, in G major”). Add replaces the example chart (or starts an empty one, guessing the key from chord names), and otherwise puts the chords at the end.
  • Tap a chord in the key: the seven chords of the key at the selected chord, with their numerals; tick 7ths for seventh chords. Each tap plays the chord and adds it after the selected one, or into it if it is an empty slot. The number keys 1–7 do the same from the keyboard.
  • Name a shape on guitar or piano opens the Chord finder; MIDI in in Chart tools takes chords from a MIDI keyboard (see More tools).

The same numerals and Nashville numbers work in Edit as text, in each part’s own key after a [Key: …] line.

Starting fresh: Generate

Open Create → Generate. (To start from a tune instead, see Harmonize a melody.)

  • Mood sets the feeling: Uplifting, Melancholy, Dreamy, Dark, Tense, Nostalgic, Epic or Mysterious. Pick one, or none.
  • Genre sets the style: Pop, Rock, Blues, Jazz, R&B / Neo-soul, Gospel, Funk, Country, Folk, EDM, Metal, Bossa nova, Lo-fi and Film score. Pick several to blend them.
  • What this genre plays shows each picked genre’s typical chords in your key; tap one to hear it. Chords shown faded are outside Chords allowed. Turn on Let the genre choose its chords to use them anyway.
  • Predictable ↔ Surprising controls how often a chord from outside the key sneaks in.
  • Length and Chord changes set how many bars and how often the chord changes. Keep my mode stops Generate from switching to another mode.
  • Press Generate. The box above the button explains in plain words what Generate is about to do, and the new progression plays straight away.

Giving an existing chart a genre’s sound

Pick one or more genres, then press Restyle my chart in this style in the “What this genre plays” box. Each chord keeps its root, bass note and length, but takes on the genre’s colours: 7th chords for blues, 9ths and 13ths for jazz, power chords for rock and metal, and so on. Locked chords stay as they are, and Undo puts everything back. (Apply chords allowed in Chart tools is different: it recolours the chart to the richest chord types your limits allow, without any genre.)

Keeping the chords you like

Select a chord and press Lock in the Chord panel (a small padlock appears on it in the chart). Regenerate unlocked then refills only the other chords and keeps every chord’s length. Empty slots (shown as ?) are always refilled.

Key, meter and limits

The key line under the chart title (for example “G major · 4/4 · 4 bars”) opens the key, mode and time signature; tempo is in the Play bar, where you can type it or nudge it with − and +. Create → Chords allowed sets the most colourful chord types you want anywhere in the app. Generate and Harmonize both follow these settings. The presets Simple, Color, Jazz and Everything are starting points; tap the families (Power, Sus, Add & 6, 7ths, 9 · 11 · 13, Altered, Dim & aug, Slash) to fine-tune. Plain major and minor chords are always allowed. Chords allowed sets how far the app may go, not how far it must: with 9ths allowed you still get plenty of maj7 and m7 chords, and the picker shows the key’s 7ths and 9ths in separate rows. Only Apply chords allowed deliberately rewrites every chord to the richest type allowed.

Changing key or mode part-way through

Select the first chord of the bar where the new key starts, and in the Chord panel choose the key and mode under Key from bar …. It works for a bridge in a new key, a last-chorus lift up a step, or a switch of mode on the same home note (C major to C Mixolydian, say). From that bar on, the chart’s colours, Roman numerals, swap ideas, the chord picker and note spelling all follow the new key, and the panel says in words what kind of move it is. A label such as Key: D major marks the change in the chart, and the key line under the title lists every key the chart passes through. Choose No change to remove it.

Key changes go with the chart when you transpose, save, share, print or export MusicXML (which gets a new key signature at that bar). Regenerate unlocked keeps them and writes the new chords in each part’s key; Generate starts a fresh chart in one key.

Key centres you don’t write down

Jazz tunes often change key every bar or two without a written key change, each new key reached through its own V7 or a ii–V. With Find key centres on (in Chart tools; it starts on), the app spots this: when a V7 resolves to a major or minor chord outside the key, and there is a ii before it or the chart does it to two or more different chords, that stretch is read in the key it’s heading for, through any chords that belong to that key. Where a centre starts, the numeral is prefixed with its key, as in Roman-numeral analysis: G: V7 I. The summary under the chart names the centres, and calls them Coltrane changes when they are a major third apart.

It leaves alone what isn’t a key change: a single V7 into a borrowed chord in a pop song, borrowing from the parallel minor, and a minor 7th that is only the ii of the next ii–V in a chain. A key change you write yourself always wins.

Trying it with Giant Steps. In I have chords (or Edit as text), type one bar between each |; two chords in a bar share it, two beats each:

B D7 | G Bb7 | Eb | Am7 D7
G Bb7 | Eb F#7 | B | Fm7 Bb7
Eb | Am7 D7 | G | C#m7 F#7
B | Fm7 Bb7 | Eb | C#m7 F#7

The tune is played at about 280 beats a minute, faster than the app’s top tempo of 240; set 140 and hear it at half speed, which is how it’s usually practised anyway.

Writing a chart as text

Open Edit as text under the chart to type or paste a progression, then press Load (or ⌘/Ctrl + Enter). Every change you make in the chart is written back here too.

[Verse]
C | Am | F | G:2 G7:2
[Chorus]
F | G | Em | ?
[Bridge]
[Key: A major]
D | E | F#m | E
  • One line per phrase; | between bars. Chords sharing a bar split it evenly.
  • [Verse] or Chorus: at the start of a line labels a section.
  • [Key: A major] (or [Key: Bb minor], [Key: D Dorian]) changes key from the next bar. At the very top it sets the opening key.
  • :2 after a chord gives it an exact number of beats.
  • ? is an empty slot you want ideas for; % repeats the previous chord; an empty bar | | holds the chord for another bar.
  • Chord names are read flexibly: Cmaj7, CΔ7, C-7, Cø, F#m7b5, Bbsus4, Gmaj7(9), D/F# and many more.

On a phone, and with no connection

On a phone the chart comes first. Search, Text size, Simple view, Library and Help are behind More at the top right, and Song form, the tension line and the notes follow the chords.

After your first visit the app and these help pages are kept on your device, so they open with no connection. To add it to your home screen, use your browser’s Install or Add to Home Screen. When a new version arrives, a message tells you to reload; nothing changes while you’re working.

Rhythm: grid sizes and zoom

With the melody lane on, Grid and Zoom sit just above the chart.

  • Grid sets where new notes land: quarters, 8ths, triplets or 16ths (in 6/8 and 7/8, the sizes start from 8ths). Changing it never moves a note, so finer notes stay where they are on a coarser grid.
  • Zoom shows fewer bars on each row, so you can look closely at a bar. On a narrow screen the chart may fit fewer bars than you chose, and a fine grid can scroll sideways.
  • On a finer grid each note is a block. Tap a slot, then pick a note. Longer and Shorter change its length, and typing - extends it. In Type notes, each note takes one slot, a dash extends the note before it, and a dot rests.
  • Harmonize still reads the tune in whole and half beats for now, so notes on finer positions count at the nearest half beat. Chords still start on whole beats too. The notation (MusicXML) download keeps 8ths and longer for now, so finer notes are left out of it.

Put in melody lane, in the Harmonize panel, starts at the bar you've selected and changes only the bars it covers. The panel tells you which bars before anything is written, and whether notes will be replaced or won't fit.

Break away from another song

If you started from another song and can’t get it out of your head, enter its chords (and its tune in the melody lane, if you can), then press Break away in the Song form strip. That chart is kept as the original, and a panel shows how many bars still follow it.

  • Bars that still match are tagged in the chart with what matches: chords, notes, or both.
  • Up to three bars get ideas. Try swaps in a different chord that keeps your tune’s note, Flip the tune’s shape turns that bar’s notes upside down, and Start a beat later shifts them along (a note on the last beat drops off). Undo reverses any of them.
  • Hear the original and Hear yours let you compare them.

Transposing doesn’t count as moving away, and neither does adding colour, so C and Cmaj7 count as the same chord. A bar also counts if it and the bar beside it appear together anywhere in the original, so moving a section doesn’t hide a match. Notes count as matching when three quarters of them still run in the same order, so a new rhythm alone doesn’t count as moving away.

The count measures chords and notes, not how the song sounds, so let your ear decide. It can’t tell you a song is legally safe. The original stays in this browser, and Stop comparing removes it.

Song form: verses, choruses and repeats

The Song form strip above the chart shows the sections in order, for example Verse 1 · Chorus · Verse 2 · Chorus · Bridge · Chorus. Tap one to jump to it and see what you can do with it.

  • + Name a section: select a chord at the start of a bar, then choose Intro, Verse, Pre-chorus, Chorus, Bridge or Outro, or type a name. A section runs until the next one starts. In Edit as text, the same thing is a line like [Chorus].
  • Repeat after it or Repeat at the end copies a section, with its stretch of the tune. Write a chorus once, then repeat it.
  • Drag a section to reorder the song: drag its chip in the Song form strip, or its name above its bars in the chart, and drop it among the chips or anywhere over the chart. A yellow line shows where it will land. Hold Option/Alt to copy it instead. Its stretch of the tune goes with it, and one Undo puts it back. An unnamed opening section that moves is named “Section” so it stays its own section.
  • Fold repeats: a section that matches an earlier one of the same name note for note (its chords, and its tune when the melody lane is showing) folds to one line, “Same as bar 5”, so a long song fits on screen. Show opens it, and it opens by itself when the melody cursor moves into it. “Verse 1” and “Verse 2” count as the same name.
  • Link repeats: change a chord in one Chorus and every Chorus that matched it changes too; one Undo puts them all back. With a tune in the melody lane, a change that makes the section longer or shorter stays in that one section, so the tune under the others doesn’t move. Turn Link repeats off to change just one.

The chart is still played, exported and printed in full, repeats included; folding only changes what you see.

Melody

Press ♪ Melody beside Chart tools to show a melody lane: a row of beat boxes under each line of chords, one per beat, lined up with the chords above. Press it again to hide the lane; the melody is kept.

  • Enter notes: tap a box, then a note on the pad that opens under the chart. The cursor moves to the next beat, so you can put a tune in beat by beat. On a computer, just type: A–G for a note, # or ] to raise it a half step and [ to lower it, - to hold the note before, . for a rest, ↑ ↓ for the octave, ← → to move, Delete to clear.
  • Three ways to pick a note on the pad: the note buttons (each black key shows both its names, D♭ and C♯); the Note and Octave menus, which list every spelling and change the selected beat without moving on; or Type notes, a box that takes several at once with sharps and flats written any way (F# G Bb A, E♭ D). It reads them like Harmonize does: | between bars fits each bar from the start of the selected beat’s bar, - holds, . rests and (A B) puts two notes in a beat. The line under the box says how it reads them before you press Put in (or Enter); each note goes in the octave nearest the one before, and the cursor moves on to the next beat.
  • Colours show each note against the chord above it: filled green is a chord tone, dashed green a colour note (the chord’s 9th, 11th or 13th, or another note of the key), and a red underline a clash, a note outside both on a strong beat. Clashes aren’t mistakes; they are worth hearing on purpose.
  • Hearing it: the melody plays with the chart whenever the lane is showing. Tapping a note plays it over its chord.
  • Chords for a melody: Shift-click (or Shift-arrow) to select some beats, then Suggest chords for bars … on the pad. The ideas open in Create → Harmonize, set to go over just those bars; press Add to chart to use one. The melody itself is never changed.
  • Tune ideas: with a note selected, Tune ideas for bar … on the pad suggests small changes to the notes in that bar (or Look across the whole tune). A note that clashes with its chord on a strong beat can move to the nearest chord tone above or below, or lean in: a chord tone on the beat with your note half a beat later, where it becomes a passing note. A leap of a 3rd or more can be filled with a note on the “and” of the beat before it. A note held two beats or more can step to its neighbour and back. Each idea says why; Now and With it play the bar both ways with its chords, and nothing changes until you press Use (Undo takes it back).
  • Two notes in a beat: press / (or Split the beat on the pad) to split a beat in half, eighth notes in 4/4. The cell shows both notes; tap either half to change it, and Delete on the second half joins the beat again. Arrows step through the halves.
  • In Harmonize, Put in melody lane writes a typed melody into the lane from bar 1.

Typing a tune with any number of notes per bar

In Harmonize, put | between bars and each bar’s notes are fitted into the bar, whatever their number. The result won’t be the rhythm in your head, but every note lands in its own bar and the chords line up:

  • More notes than beats: the weakest beats are split in two first, from the end of the bar back (in 4/4: beat 4, then 2, then 3, then 1), so the strong beats keep one note each. Up to two notes fit in a beat.
  • Fewer notes than beats: the notes go on the strongest beats and are held over the rest.
  • Your own grouping: brackets put two notes in one beat where you want them, for example (G# A) C# B A. A bar written with brackets is taken one beat per item, as written.
  • Without any bar lines, it is one note per beat as before.

For example, G# G# G# G# | G# A C# B A | A G# F# E C# E | A B A G# F# becomes four quarter notes; then G♯ A C♯ and B–A as two eighths; then A, G♯–F♯, E, C♯–E; then A B A and G♯–F♯.

The melody is saved with the chart and in the Library, travels in share links, and moves when you transpose. It follows time, not chords: if you move a chord, the tune stays where it is and the harmony under it changes. Clear chart clears both. MIDI and MusicXML exports include it on a track or staff of its own.

Arranging the melody for voices or strings

Press Arrange beside ♪ Melody to hear how the tune could be harmonized by a group. No tune yet? The top part then sings a line of its own, one chord tone per chord, each as near as it can be to the one before; it shows in grey in the melody lane until you write your own tune there. Three more parts appear as lanes around the melody lane, each note named with its octave, in pitch order from top to bottom, so you can see how the harmony stacks at every beat. They follow the chords in the chart and change as you write the tune.

StyleHow it stacks
ChoirFour-part writing as in a hymn: the tune on top, the bass on each chord’s root, the inner voices moving as little as they can, with no parallel fifths or octaves.
String quartetViolin 1 plays the tune and the cello the bass of each chord. Violin 2 and viola fill in, holding notes where they can and avoiding parallel fifths and octaves. The notes swell and join (legato).
BarbershopThe tune is the lead, the second voice down; the tenor sings above it, the bass takes the root or fifth and the baritone fills in. Every tune note gets its own chord: a tune note outside the chord gets a dominant 7th or diminished chord that contains it.
Close harmonyThe jazz vocal-group sound: three voices packed within an octave under the tune, with 6ths, 7ths and 9ths. A 9th in the tune replaces the root; a note a half step above a chord tone gets a passing diminished chord. Short, detached notes.
Drop 2Close harmony with the second voice down an octave: wider and warmer.
OrchestraEight parts, each with a job (below).

Arranging for orchestra

With more than four parts, arrangers don’t write every line independently: they give each section a job, and doubling is how an orchestra gets its weight. Orchestra starts from the string quartet arrangement and builds around it:

JobWho
The tuneViolins I, doubled an octave up by the flute (in unison when that would be too high).
CountermelodyClarinet, only where the tune holds still: a stepwise line onto the next chord’s 3rd, under the tune when there is room, over it when not.
PadHorn, holding a guide tone (the chord’s 3rd or 7th) and avoiding notes the strings already have; Violins II hold their line.
PulseViolas, playing their note again on every beat.
BassCellos, doubled an octave down by the double basses.

A part that only doubles another shares its lane (Vn1+Fl, Vc+Cb). Tap a note for a card that gives every section’s job at that beat, then how the strings were voiced. The score is written at concert pitch (clarinet and horn as they sound), with the double basses in bass clef an octave down.

Choosing the singers

For the vocal styles, Singers sets who sings, and so each part’s comfortable range (hover a part’s name to see it):

GroupParts, top to bottom
SATBSoprano C4–A5, Alto G3–D5, Tenor C3–G4, Bass E2–C4: a mixed choir.
TTBBTenor 1, Tenor 2, Baritone, Bass: men’s voices, the usual barbershop quartet.
SSAASoprano 1, Soprano 2, Alto 1, Alto 2: women’s voices.
Jazz SATBSATB in the narrower, higher ranges a close-harmony group sings in.

Barbershop chooses men’s or women’s voices to fit your tune unless you pick one. A note outside its singer’s range gets a red dot in its lane, and the Arrange bar names the first few, with a fix when there is one: Move the tune down an octave (or up), or Transpose to …, the key that puts the most notes in range. Both can be undone.

Why is that note there?

Tap any note in the parts’ lanes. A card opens for that moment, with its column outlined:

  • The voicing, top to bottom: each note, its job in the chord (“the 7th of G7”, “a passing note, not in C”) and how it got there (“holds: a note Am and Dm7 share”, “steps down from G4”, “leaps up a 5th from C3”).
  • Why this chord: for example “The tune’s D is not in C, so it gets G7, the chord’s own dominant”, or, in close harmony, how the chord was built (“The tune’s D is the chord’s 9th… it takes the place of the root”).
  • The next best voicing and what it loses on: moving the parts further, parallel fifths, leaving out the 3rd, a voice out of range.
  • Hear it, Hear the move (the moment before, then this one), Hear the bar (the whole bar, every part) and Hear the next best, so you can compare by ear. The arrows step through the changes.

The same few habits come up again and again: keep the notes two chords share, move by the smallest step, keep the 3rd and 7th, avoid parallel fifths and octaves, and treat a tune note outside the chord as a reason for a passing chord. Those are what an arranger listens for too.

  • Try another gives a different arrangement in the same style; First version goes back to the first one.
  • Hearing the parts: Play (in the Arrange bar or the Play bar) plays every part with the chart’s chords. Tap a part’s name to mute it (its lane greys out), or S to hear it alone. Muting Piano chords leaves just the voices. While the arrangement is showing, tapping a chord in the chart plays that chord’s stretch of the arrangement, every part you haven’t muted, instead of the chord alone.
  • Taking it with you: Export → Download arrangement (MusicXML) writes each part on its own staff in its usual clef (violins and upper voices in treble, viola in alto, tenors in treble an octave down, cello, baritone and bass in bass clef) with chord symbols over the top. MIDI exports put each part on its own track.

The sounds differ only in how notes start and end; they are there to judge the harmony, not to sound like real players. The parts are written at the octave you wrote the tune in, so a low tune gives low parts.

Swap ideas

Select a chord and open the Swap ideas tab. Ideas are grouped (chords that do the same job, recolourings, borrowed chords, chords that lead into the next one, and more). Each idea shows:

  • its theory name, such as “Secondary dominant (V7/vi)” or “Modal interchange”;
  • a sentence about this exact swap: which notes it keeps, whether it sounds brighter or darker, and where it goes next;
  • a voice-leading line: which notes are held and how far each voice moves from the chord before;
  • ▶ to hear it in context, A/B to loop the original and the idea bar by bar, and Put in this slot to use it.

Hearing it in context. ▶ plays the chord or chords before it, then the idea, then the chord after. The menu at the top of Swap ideas sets how far back it starts: the chord before, 2 chords or 4 chords. Playback never wraps round the ends of the chart: an idea for the last chord stops on the idea, and one for the first chord starts on it. (The ideas themselves still treat the chart as a loop, since a progression usually repeats.)

Ideas for an empty slot. An empty slot gets an After … group built from the chord before it: after G7, its resolution (Cm7 in C minor), the deceptive move to A♭maj7, a chain on to C7, and the same landing in the other quality. At the end of the chart this group comes first, and the chords that lead back to bar 1 come later, labelled Lead back to … at the top, since bar 1 only follows when the chart loops. Mid-chart, the key’s chords, ranked against the chords on both sides, come first.

Keep note lists every chord containing a note you choose, for example your melody note: every root and every chord type that Chords allowed switches on, and, when slash chords are on, slash chords with that note in the bass (C/G, Am/G, F/G and the rest). The likeliest come first: chords in the key, then borrowed and secondary chords, then chromatic ones, the best of each root before the second best of any. The first 8 show, and Show more opens 24 at a time. When two names mean the same notes over the same bass, the plainer one is listed.

The Chords allowed setting is named at the top of Swap ideas; tap it to change which chord families ideas can use.

Ideas for this progression

Under the chart, three swap ideas from across the whole chart, each of a different kind (borrowed, leading into the next chord, recolouring, chromatic, jazz) and each for a different chord where the chart allows. ▶ plays it in context, Use swaps it in (Undo takes it back), and Other ideas shows the next three. They are the same ideas as in Swap ideas, so a chord’s full list is a tap away. They hide, with the sentence about the progression, while Quiz me is on.

Playing and hearing

  • Play in the bottom bar plays the chart; Loop repeats it. Space also starts and stops playback. Changes you make while it plays (adding or swapping a chord, the tempo, the style) are heard from the next beat, without stopping.
  • Sound: Piano or Guitar. Guitar plays the actual guitar shapes in your tuning and capo.
  • Style: piano can play block chords, a ballad (bass, then chords), a pop pulse or an arpeggio, or one of the two piano parts below; guitar can strum once, play a strum pattern (down, down-up, up-down-up) or fingerpick.
  • Smooth voice leading (in Chart tools) keeps the piano’s notes close from chord to chord; Fixed root position plays every chord the plain way.
  • The metronome button next to the tempo adds a click on every beat (accented on the downbeat), from the next pass through the chart.
  • In a meter made of groups (5/4, 6/8, 7/8, 9/8, 12/8), block chords and a single strum are struck again, more softly, at the start of each group (7/8 as 2+2+3), so you hear the meter, not just a longer bar. The metronome accents the same groups.

Broadway and jazz piano

Two piano styles play a whole part rather than one pattern per chord, because what they do depends on the chord coming next and on what the tune in the melody lane is doing. Choose them in the Style menu with the Piano sound. Two lanes appear under the chart, LH and RH, naming what each hand does; tap one to read why, then Hear it or Hear it plain (the same bars as block chords) to compare.

Broadway: stride and fillsJazz ballad: rootless voicings
Stride: the left hand plays bass on the strong beats and a chord in the middle of the keyboard between (in 3/4, a waltz). Plain major chords get a 6th.Rootless voicings: the root low and soft, then the 3rd, 7th, 9th and 5th or 13th in the left hand, in whichever of the two standard forms moves least.

With no tune in the melody lane, the fills come at the ends of two-bar phrases. MIDI export writes the part as planned. These are sketches of the styles, built from a few of their habits, not of any particular pianist or song.

Guitar and piano shapes

The Chord tab shows the chord’s notes, guitar shapes and piano voicings. The shape marked Smoothest path is the one playback uses, chosen so your hand moves as little as possible. Tap any shape or voicing to hear it, then ▶ In context to hear it between its neighbours or Use for this chord to make playback use it. A pinned shape is played whichever sound is chosen: a guitar shape you pinned is what the piano plays too, unless that chord also has a piano pin. The Broadway and Jazz ballad styles write their own voicings, so they don’t use pins.

Under each guitar box are the notes that sound, and the green bass and gold right-hand notes of each piano voicing are written out underneath it. With a capo, the box shows the capo as a gold bar marked CAPO and its fret, fret numbers count from the capo, and the note names are the sounding notes; the Smoothest path badge names the capo it was worked out for. Press Zoom beside Guitar or Piano to see every diagram at about twice the size (press Smaller to go back); the choice is remembered.

Pick a Tuning (standard, half or whole step down, Drop D, Drop C, double drop D, DADGAD, open G, D, E or C) and a Capo. Find the easiest tuning for this chart compares every tuning and capo position for your chords and recommends one, keeping standard tuning unless another is clearly easier.

More tools

  • Chord finder (under the chart): place fingers on a fretboard or press piano keys, and it names the chord and shows it on the other instrument.
  • Harmonize a melody (in Create → Harmonize): type one note per beat (for example E D C D | E E E -, with - to hold and . for a rest) and get three to five progressions, ranked by how many strong-beat melody notes are chord tones. It uses the chart’s key and time, and Chords allowed. Choose where the chords go under Put the chords: at the end of the chart (the default, which keeps everything already there), before the selected chord, over the selected chord and the bars after it, or in place of the whole chart. Then press Add to chart; the message that appears has an Undo button.
  • Play chords in from a MIDI keyboard (Chrome, Edge and other browsers with Web MIDI; not Safari): open Chart tools and set MIDI in to your keyboard. In other browsers Chart tools says so instead. A Live bar appears above the Play bar and names whatever you hold, in large letters, as you play: its numeral and what it does in the key at the selected chord (its job, in Simple view), coloured the way the chart is, the keys on a small keyboard, and other names when the same notes could be read two ways (C6 is also Am7/C). The name waits a moment for the chord to settle, so pressing keys one by one doesn’t flicker. Notes held by the sustain pedal count until you lift it. After you let go, the last chord stays, marked Last, and Add to chart puts it after the selected chord. The chord you let go of also appears in the Chord finder with every name it could have. Hear what I play (on to start with) sounds each key through the app as you play, in the Play bar’s Piano or Guitar sound, as loud as you strike it, for as long as you hold it or the sustain pedal holds it; turn it off if your keyboard makes its own sound. Nothing you play goes into the chart until you press Add to chart, which adds the chord a bar long after the selected chord (or into it, if it is an empty slot). The lowest note you play becomes the bass, so playing E under a C chord gives C/E.
  • Keys and modes: the chords in your key, and how well your chart fits each mode on the same tonic. Click a chord to hear it; it goes into the chart only when you press Put in bar … (replacing the selected chord) or Add after it.
  • Tension curve above the chart shows how much each chord pulls away from home. It is a map, not a score: higher is not better. Most songs rise and settle, and a calm progression of three plain chords is as good as a restless one full of borrowed and altered chords. Use it to see what you made and to try things, not as a target.
  • Show voice leading draws each voice’s path through the chart, marking held notes and big leaps.
  • Quiz me hides the colours and labels and asks what each chord does; your score is kept.

Rhythmic cycles and drones

The time signature menu also offers Carnatic and Hindustani cycles (Adi talam, Rupaka, Misra and Khanda chapu, Teental, Jhaptal, Rupak taal, Ektal) as grids for placing chord changes, and Create offers a Sa–Pa drone under a mode. These are sketching aids, not authoritative accounts of those traditions.

If you hear nothing

  • Phones and browsers only allow sound after you tap the page, so the first tap on Play is what starts it.
  • Check the volume and where the sound is going (Bluetooth, headphones).
  • Open Help → Sound check. It plays two test beeps by different routes and shows what your device reports, which narrows down where the sound stops.

Your data

Everything stays in your browser: your current chart, library, settings and quiz score are saved in this browser’s local storage on this device. There are no accounts and no tracking. Clearing your browser data clears them, so export the library now and then if it matters to you.
